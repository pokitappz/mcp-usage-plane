Security

What we hold, what we cannot hold, and what we do not do yet.

A security page made of reassuring adjectives is worth nothing. This one names the actual mechanisms, and it ends with a list of things this service does not do, because a page that only lists strengths tells you the author was selling rather than describing.

Your customers' data

There is nowhere in our database to put it.

We store counts, not content. A usage record is seven columns: an identifier, which account it belongs to, which of your customers it was for, a label naming what was used, how many units, and two timestamps. That is the entire row.

There is no column for the name of a tool, what was asked, what came back, or which file was read. Not as a policy that could be changed later, but as a fact about the shape of the table: those things could not be stored here even by accident.

Two of those columns are yours, and that is a real constraint on you. The customer identifier and the label are whatever your server sends. Send an opaque identifier and a label you chose, and nothing identifying ever leaves your side. Put a customer's email address in the label and it will be stored, because this service cannot tell the difference. Saying otherwise would be the dishonest version of this paragraph.

The meter on your side is stricter still. What it reports for monitoring never includes API keys, customer identifiers, method names or tool names, so the reporting path does not quietly put back what the database declined to hold.

Keys and passwords

Nothing reusable is stored in a form anyone can read back.

Five separate things, each protected differently, and none of them recoverable from our side.

We refuse the dangerous kind of Stripe key

We only accept a restricted key, and we check. Paste an ordinary secret key and the service turns it down and tells you how to create the right one. Stripe describes giving a secret key to a third party as dangerous, and they are right. Keys beginning rk_ only. Checked when you save the setting and again before every request, so a key written straight into the database still cannot be used.

And we encrypt the one we do accept

Encrypted before it is written down, with a fresh random value each time so two identical keys do not look identical in storage. ChaCha20-Poly1305 with a 12 byte nonce per value, stored as enc:<base64(nonce||ciphertext)>. A key that is configured but malformed stops the service starting rather than quietly storing the next credential unprotected.

API keys are stored as fingerprints

We keep a one-way fingerprint, never the key itself, so somebody who obtained a copy of the database would not get a working credential from it. Nothing in the system can print one back to you after it is created; if you lose one, you replace it. SHA-256 lookup digests, for account tokens, customer API keys and browser sessions alike.

Charges are never sent somewhere else

When we send usage to your payment provider, the request will not follow a redirect to another address, and the destination is checked against a list first. A redirect is the easy way to walk a credential somewhere nobody reviewed.

There is no password to steal

Signing in emails you a six digit code. There is no password to reuse, leak or reset. The resulting session can be revoked from our side, unlike a token that simply expires whenever it feels like it. Cookie is HttpOnly and SameSite=Lax, and Secure wherever the service is reachable over https.

The question worth asking

What could somebody who stole our database do to your Stripe account?

This is the question to ask any company that connects to your payments, and most of them answer it with a certification logo. Here is the actual answer.

They could add usage that did not happen. That is the whole list. The key we hold can write usage events and nothing else: it cannot read your customers, take a payment, issue a refund, or change where your money is paid out. Invented usage also shows up quickly, because we keep the record of what your own servers reported and the two would stop matching.

That is not nothing, and we are not going to pretend it is. It is recoverable in a way the alternative is not: if we accepted an ordinary Stripe secret key, the honest answer to the question above would be anything at all, and no part of that would be undoable.

Lock the key to our servers

Stripe lets you attach an access policy to a key so it only works from certain places. Set one and a stolen key is useless anywhere else. We will publish the addresses to allow once this service is deployed; today you can already restrict it by country and block anonymising networks.

Or do not give us a key at all

We can send your usage to your own server instead, signed so you can check it came from us, and you make the Stripe call yourself. It is more work for you, and it is the right answer if handing over any credential is off the table. Ask and we will set it up.

Or run the whole thing yourself

This service is source-available. You can read every line of it, deploy it against your own database, and point it at your own Stripe account, at which point none of this page applies to you: there is no third party, because you are the operator. The licence lets you run it for your own billing and becomes Apache-2.0 two years after each release.

These pages

The site enforces its own rules, and a test proves it.

Every page we serve tells your browser to refuse any code that did not come from us, and we hold ourselves to that: there is not one line of script or styling embedded in the pages themselves. That is what allows the rule to be set as strictly as it is.

It is enforced rather than intended. A test renders every page on this site and fails the build if an inline script, style or handler appears in any of them, because a rule the pages quietly break is worse than no rule: it looks like protection and reports nothing. A content security policy with no unsafe-inline and no unsafe-eval, plus frame-ancestors 'none' and form-action 'self'.

Anything that changes your data is checked against where the request came from, and a request that does not say is refused rather than assumed friendly. Dashboard pages are marked so that no shared cache or proxy ever keeps a copy of one account's figures.

Limits

Every way in has a ceiling on it.

Mostly so that one badly behaved client, or somebody deliberately trying, cannot degrade the service for everyone else.

Checking our work

The part that decides what you owe is open source.

The rules that decide what counts as billable are not hidden in this service. They live in open source code anyone can read, and the behaviour is pinned by a published set of test cases in plain JSON that software written in any language can run against.

If you think something was billed that should not have been, that is a question you can settle by reading the code, rather than a support ticket you have to win.

What we do not do

Things worth knowing before you decide.

This is a young service. All of the following are true today, and they are here so you can weigh them now rather than find them later.

  • One location. A single deployment and a single database. You cannot choose where your data lives, and there is no automatic failover. An outage here is an outage for everyone, which is exactly why your own server is built to keep serving customers from its cached copy without us.
  • We do not cut off unpaid accounts. The service records whether an account is paying, past due or cancelled, and nothing reads that to refuse service. A cancelled account carries on working. That is deliberate for now, and it is written here because assuming the opposite is the expensive mistake.
  • We hold the encryption key. You cannot bring your own. Encrypting your Stripe key protects you if the database is stolen; it does not protect you from us. What limits that is the key itself rather than the encryption: we only accept one that can write usage events, so the most anyone here could do with it is record usage that did not happen. Attach a Stripe access policy and even that stops working from anywhere but our servers.
  • No record of who changed what. Sign-ins identify a person, and nothing currently writes down who changed a price or revoked a key. The database was built to carry that; the recording is not written yet.
  • Nobody independent has audited this. There has been no external security assessment. When that changes it will be said here, with a date and a name, and not before. In the meantime the code is readable, which is not the same as audited but is more than most services in this position will offer you.

Found something wrong? Email support@pokitapps.com. Anything about the open source code belongs in its published security policy.
